# [H-01] An ERC20 allowance over ANTIMATTER is silently a dual-asset authority

**Ledger fingerprint:** `033432b0e650af67`
**Severity:** High
**Location:** `lib/antimatter/src/Antimatter.sol:214`, `:215`, `:222`, `:236`, `:237` (function body `:201-239`)
**Root cause class:** `SingleAllowanceGrantsDualAssetAuthority`

## Finding description and impact

### Summary

`annihilateFrom` spends exactly one authority — the caller's **antimatter** allowance over `from` (`Antimatter.sol:214`) — but performs **two** debits against `from`: the antimatter burn (`:215`) and a pull of `from`'s **stablecoin** (`:222`). It then delivers the entire ~2x phUSD proceeds of both debits to a caller-supplied `recipient` (`:236`, `:237`) that is validated only against `address(0)` (`:203`).

The consequence is that `antimatter.approve(bob, N)` is, in effect, also an approval for Bob to spend `toStableAmount(N)` of **every stablecoin the grantor has approved to Antimatter**, plus an unrestricted right to redirect the resulting phUSD to Bob himself. The grantor receives nothing.

### Vulnerability details

The relevant sequence in `annihilateFrom` (`lib/antimatter/src/Antimatter.sol:214-237`):

```solidity
if (from != msg.sender) _spendAllowance(from, msg.sender, amount);   // :214  antimatter only
_burn(from, amount);                                                  // :215

IERC20(stable).safeTransferFrom(from, address(this), stableAmount);   // :222  SECOND asset, no authority checked
IERC20(stable).forceApprove(address(minter), stableAmount);
minter.mint(stable, stableAmount);                                    // :227
...
_phUSD.mint(recipient, amount);                                       // :236  antimatter leg -> caller-chosen address
IERC20(address(_phUSD)).safeTransfer(recipient, mintedForStable);     // :237  stable leg   -> caller-chosen address
```

`_spendAllowance` at `:214` is the **only** allowance spend in the function; the stablecoin at `:222` moves on Antimatter's own standing approval from `from`, and no `IERC20(stable).allowance(from, msg.sender)` is ever read or debited. Verified by re-reading the function at the audited commit.

Attack path, with no privilege, no flash loan, no timing or price condition:

1. The victim approves Antimatter to spend her stablecoin. This is **mandatory**, not a coincidence an attacker waits for: `:222` pulls the stablecoin from `from`, so no holder can ever annihilate without it. A max approval — the normal way to use such a contract more than once — makes the exposure her entire balance.
2. The victim grants an ordinary ERC20 antimatter allowance to a third party (a keeper, router, or counterparty). This is a shipped, documented, unit-tested flow (see *Prior art in the project's own tests* below).
3. The attacker calls `annihilateFrom(stable, victim, attacker, amount)`. Line `:214` spends only the antimatter allowance; `:215` burns the victim's antimatter.
4. Line `:222` debits the victim's stablecoin.
5. Lines `:236`/`:237` deliver the whole ~2x phUSD to `attacker`.
6. Repeatable up to the granted antimatter allowance.

### Impact

Direct, uncompensated theft of a named victim's assets by a named attacker in a single transaction. The victim loses her antimatter **and** her entire stablecoin balance approved to Antimatter; the attacker receives ~2x that value in phUSD. The victim's stablecoin allowance to the attacker is zero throughout and stays zero — nothing about the loss is visible in the approvals the victim can inspect.

This meets the C4 High definition literally: assets are stolen directly, via a concrete attack path, with no hypotheticals.

### Prior art in the project's own tests — stated honestly

The project's test suite asserts that the current behaviour is **correct**. `lib/antimatter/test/Annihilation.t.sol:129-141`, `test_thirdPartySpendsAntimatterAllowance`, grants `spender` an antimatter allowance, has `spender` call `annihilateFrom(usdc, user, recipient, 60 ether)`, and asserts `phUSD.balanceOf(recipient) == 120 ether` — i.e. that a third party may move a holder's stablecoin and route the doubled proceeds to a different address. The NatSpec at `:189-192` documents the same intent: *"When `from` is not the caller, the caller's antimatter allowance over `from` is spent exactly as {transferFrom} would spend it. The stablecoin is always pulled from `from`."*

This is not a defence, for a reason that is precise rather than rhetorical: **an ERC20 approver cannot consent to the movement of a second asset she was never asked about.** `approve(spender, N)` on the antimatter contract is a statement about antimatter and nothing else. ERC20 authority is per token, and every wallet, allowance dashboard and revocation tool a user might consult presents it that way. There is no interface at which the victim is shown, or could infer, that the antimatter grant carries her USDC with it. Documenting a behaviour in the callee's NatSpec does not transfer consent to the approver, and in-source documentation carries no suppression authority. Under Law 1, an intended-but-exploitable design is reportable.

Two related C4 known-invalid categories were checked and do **not** apply. *User error / phishing*: the victim's actions are exactly what the documentation and tests instruct, and the harm is invisible at approval time. *Approve race / safeApprove front-running*: nothing is racing here; the authority is over-broad by construction, not by ordering.

### Severity justification

**High, sustained** after explicitly testing the "no hypotheticals" bar. Neither precondition is extraordinary: the stablecoin approval to Antimatter is a hard requirement of ever annihilating, so it is universal among users rather than an attacker-awaited coincidence; and an antimatter allowance is a plain ERC20 `approve` that the project ships as a supported flow. No owner action, no third contract, no market condition and no timing dependency is involved. Executable at will by any allowance grantee.

## Recommended mitigation

**Primary fix — zero functional cost:**

```solidity
if (from != msg.sender && recipient != from) revert RecipientMustBeFrom();
```

The exploit requires **both** conditions simultaneously: a third-party caller (`msg.sender != from`) **and** a redirected recipient (`recipient != from`). Each alone is a legitimate flow that the project tests separately:

- `msg.sender != from` with `recipient == from` — a keeper or router annihilating on a holder's behalf and delivering to that holder. Preserved.
- `msg.sender == from` with `recipient != from` — a holder annihilating her own position to an address of her choosing. Preserved.

Only the conjunction is closed, so no shipped capability is lost. Note that `test_thirdPartySpendsAntimatterAllowance` (`test/Annihilation.t.sol:129-141`) passes `recipient` distinct from `user` and would need updating; that test currently encodes the vulnerability as expected behaviour.

**Alternatives**, if third-party redirection must be retained:

- Require and spend a real second authority: `IERC20(stable).allowance(from, msg.sender) >= stableAmount`, debited like any allowance. This makes the dual-asset nature explicit and consentable, at the cost of a second approval per user.
- Drop the `from` parameter entirely and make annihilation self-only. Simplest and safest, but removes the keeper flow.

Whichever is chosen, the NatSpec at `:189-192` should state plainly that the stablecoin leg moves on Antimatter's standing approval and not on the caller's allowance.
